Nnọọ · welcome

A language
is a homecoming.

Hear it from the people who carried it here.

Nnọọ means welcome

More than a language app.

It is a place where an elder's voice can travel across generations.

Where a child in London can hear the same word their grandmother learned in Nnewi.

Where a proverb is not just translated, but heard from someone who has carried it all their life.

Where a community decides what should be remembered, what should remain among kin, and what should remain sacred.

Two hands, one body

Nnọọ has two hands,
one body.

The right hand — learning

Aka nri

A daily practice built around real Igbo voices, dialects and tones.

The left hand — Ọbá, the archive

Aka ekpe

A living storehouse of stories, songs, recipes, proverbs, craft and place-lore, recorded from elders with consent, provenance and fair payment.

Between the two hands are two communal spaces

The council

Ikpe

The people decide what enters the archive.

The gathering

Ọha

The people find one another, wherever they live.

Remember→Record→Decide→Keep→Learn→Gather→Remember again.

No. 01 — The idea

A language does not disappear all at once.

Sometimes it disappears one word at a time.

  • IA child knows the greeting but not the answer.
  • IIA grandchild knows the proverb but not the story behind it.
  • IIIA family remembers there was a song, but nobody remembers all the words.

And the deepest knowledge often remains with the people who have carried it longest: the elders. Nnọọ creates a bridge between those elders and the generations that come after them.

Not by turning culture into a museum. By letting it remain alive.

Who taught you your first Igbo word?

No. 02 — The problem

The distance between generations is growing.

Igbo is spoken by tens of millions of people, yet many children growing up in cities and across the diaspora inherit only fragments of the language.

The knowledge is there.The people are there.The desire is there.

What is missing is the bridge.

Language apps

Habits, not voices

They create habits, but rarely teach Igbo through the voices of the people who actually carry its dialects.

YouTube

Teachers, not a path

Wonderful teachers — but no structured path through a language and no permanent community-owned archive.

Academic archives

Preserved, not shared

Preservation without participation can feel distant, difficult and extractive.

Families & Saturday schools

Warm, not lasting

They create warmth and connection. But what happens when the teacher moves away?

Nnọọ is designed around a different question

What if learning the language and preserving the culture were the same journey?

No. 03 — The Nnọọ world

Not one feature. A small cultural ecosystem.

The right hand — learning

Aka nri

Five-minute lessons. Real elders. Real voices. Real dialects.

Hear the word. See its tone. Say it yourself. Listen back. Try again.

No pressure. No artificial perfection. Just the gradual feeling of a word becoming yours.

The left hand — Ọbá

Aka ekpe

Ọbá means storehouse. Here live the things that should not be lost:

Stories. Proverbs. Songs. Recipes. Craft. Place-lore. Memories.

Every recording carries its story of origin — who spoke it, where it came from, who recorded it, who validated it, how the community decided to keep it and how the elder was paid.

The council

Ikpe

Nothing simply appears in the archive.

The community listens. Kin discuss. Elders contribute their knowledge. Then the people decide.

Igbo enwe eze — the Igbo have no king. Nnọọ turns that principle into product behaviour: no single person owns the final decision.

The gathering

Ọha

Culture is not only something we preserve. It is something we return to.

Ọha connects people by hometown, city and community. Find your people. Find the elders. Find the next story. Help record it.

No. 04 — The central loop

A loop, not a feed.

  1. 01

    Invite

    A family, hometown union or Ọha circle puts forward an elder.

  2. 02

    Consent

    The elder chooses what can be shared.

    Ọha · publicUmunna · kinNsọ · sacred
  3. 03

    Record

    A trained facilitator records the elder in their own environment, at their own pace.

  4. 04

    Send to Ikpe

    The recording does not go directly into the archive. It goes to the people.

  5. 05

    Decide

    Kin listen. They discuss. They vote.

  6. 06

    Keep

    When the community passes it, the recording enters Ọbá. The elder is paid. The provenance is completed.

  7. 07

    Teach

    The recording becomes part of the learning world. A word. A proverb. A story. A voice.

  8. 08

    Return

    Learners discover their hometown, meet their kin, sponsor another recording, nominate another elder. And the circle begins again.

Nsọ

  • A voice you recognise.
  • A town you know.
  • A word your grandmother used.
  • A proverb you have heard your whole life but never understood.

  • A recording that makes you realise: someone from my hometown was here before me.

No. 15 — The business

The archive is a public good.

Nnọọ is a mission-first hybrid. Learning and community participation help fund it.

The people create the knowledge.

The community protects it.

The product helps it travel.

The business keeps the system alive.

Revenue can come from

  • Learner subscriptions
  • Family plans
  • Grants and foundations
  • Community-sponsored recording sessions
  • Institutional licences
  • Donations

No. 16 — Where the money goes

  • The elderWhen a recording passes Ikpe, the elder is paid.
  • Field workFacilitators, travel and equipment.
  • Archive careTranscription, translation, validation and storage.
  • ProductLearning technology, pitch feedback and infrastructure.
  • CommunityHometown circles, organisers and events.
  • Future generationsRecording knowledge that might otherwise disappear.

The culture itself is not the product being sold.

  • Recordings are not sold.
  • Personal data is not sold.
  • Archive access is not quietly licensed away.
  • No external AI is trained on elder voices without explicit consent from the people and communities involved.

No. 18 — Governance

Governed with the same care with which it records.

Staff can pause a recording for safety, legal or consent reasons. But staff do not get a secret publish button. The community decides what enters the archive.

An elder advisory council helps shape

  • Consent
  • Ikpe rules
  • Cultural protocols
  • Custodian roles
  • Sacred knowledge
  • Dialect representation
  • Product language
  • Annual governance review

No. 19 — The facilitator

The bridge.

A grandchild. A neighbour. A community volunteer. A trusted member of a hometown union. Their tablet becomes a field notebook.

  • Quiet room
  • Mic ready
  • Consent recorded
  • Topic chosen
  • Listen back · check metadata · confirm provenance · confirm payment

“Take your time. There is no hurry.”

Then: Now the people decide. The facilitator does not publish. They carry the recording to the threshold.

No. 20 — The elder

The source.

The elder is never just a content creator.

  • They choose what to share.
  • They choose how it is shared.
  • They choose the consent tier.
  • They can change that decision.
  • They are named. They are paid.

Their dialect is not corrected into somebody else's. Their voice is not turned into synthetic training material without permission. Their recording is not reduced to an asset ID.

A person gave us something.

No. 21 — The technology

Technology should disappear when it is doing its job.

  • On the devicePitch feedback works on-device wherever possible.
  • OfflineA facilitator can spend 30 minutes with an elder without worrying about network coverage.
  • PreservedAudio kept at high quality.
  • RespectfulAccess always respects the recording's tier. Nsọ audio never becomes reachable through an API.
  • PrivateLearner attempts are not stored unless explicitly saved.

The technology exists to protect the human experience. Not to become the experience.

No. 23 — The first communities

Not everywhere. Meaningful somewhere.

Begin with a small number of hometowns. Record the elders. Build the circles. Test Ikpe with real people. Learn how kin verification works. Understand how dialect boundaries actually work. Watch where trust breaks. Fix it. Then expand.

Town by town, voice by voice.

No. 25 — The roadmap

Foundation

Build the trust architecture. Consent. Elder council. Custodians. Facilitator training. First recordings.

Pilot

A small number of states and hometowns. Real Ikpe decisions. Real elders paid. Learn from real communities.

Launch

Open the learning product. Introduce pitch feedback. Build Ọha circles in diaspora cities.

Scale

Expand across communities. Sponsored sessions. Schools and institutions. Let the network grow from the communities themselves.

No. 26 — What Nnọọ should become

Not the biggest language app. A place where a people can continue speaking to themselves across time.

A child hears an elder from their hometown. A family preserves a grandparent's voice. A community decides what belongs to it.

And an archive that does not only remember the past — it helps the future recognise itself.

Language is not a subject. It is inheritance.
