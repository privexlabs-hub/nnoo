Nnọọ — business brief

A voice-first platform that records the living culture of Ndị Igbo from elders, keeps it with its provenance, and teaches it to the diaspora with honest pronunciation feedback. Two hands, one body.

How to read this

This brief explains the whole project end to end: the problem, the product and every feature, how facilitators, learners and elders use it, who it serves, how it makes and spends money, how it reaches people, how it is governed, and what happens next. Anything marked Validate is a working assumption, not a verified fact — it must be tested before being quoted externally.

01Executive summary

Nnọọ (Igbo: welcome) is two products that feed each other:

  1. Ọbá — the archive. Trained community facilitators sit with Igbo elders and record language, proverbs, stories, songs, recipes, ceremonies and place-histories. Every recording is stored with full provenance — who, where, when, which dialect — and with the elder's written consent and co-ownership.
  2. Nnọọ Learn — the app. Short lessons built from those real recordings teach diaspora children and adults to speak Igbo, with tonal pronunciation feedback so learners hear and correct the tones that change meaning.

A third layer, Ọha — the community, connects diaspora Igbo by state, LGA, hometown and dialect, and is the main engine that recruits new elders and facilitators back into the archive.

The bet: the diaspora wants to learn from its own people, not from a generic flash-card app — and the act of learning can fund and grow the act of preserving.

02The problem

Language is passing faster than it is being passed on

Igbo is spoken by tens of millions of people, yet in many families — especially urban and diaspora families — children grow up understanding a few greetings and little else. The people who hold the deepest knowledge of dialect, proverb, ceremony and local history are elders, and that knowledge is rarely recorded in a form the next generation can use. Validate speaker numbers and intergenerational-transmission data before external use.

Why tone matters

Igbo is tonal: the same letters carry different meanings depending on pitch. Text-only learning cannot teach this. Learners need to hear real speakers and get feedback on their own voice — which is exactly what an elder archive plus pronunciation feedback provides.

04How it works, end to end

The whole business is one loop. Each step produces the input for the next.

01 · RECRUITSocial and hometown unions surface a family with an elder willing to share.
02 · CONSENTA facilitator explains ownership; elder signs co-ownership consent.
03 · RECORDFacilitator Mode captures audio + who / where / when / dialect.
04 · JUDGE & KEEPKin from the same hometown review it in Ikpe; once passed, it is stored in Ọbá with provenance.
05 · TEACHWords, phrases and proverbs become lessons in Nnọọ Learn.
06 · LEARNLearners practise and get tonal feedback against real voices.
07 · GATHERLearners find their hometown in Ọha and share progress with family.
08 · RETURNFamilies put forward their own elders — and the loop restarts.

Figure 1 — The preservation–learning loop. Social media feeds steps 01, 07 and 08.

What a recording contains

  • Audio — the original, never overwritten.
  • Provenance — elder's name, year of birth (or estimate), town, LGA, state, dialect; facilitator's name; date and place.
  • Consent — signed co-ownership record and the elder's chosen visibility (public, community-only, family-only).
  • Transcript & translation — Igbo with diacritics, English translation, gloss, reviewer name.
  • Tags — pillar (proverb, song, recipe, ceremony, history), topic, and lesson links.

Ikpe — the council

"Igbo enwe eze." — The Igbo have no king.

Ikpe (judgement) is where the community decides what enters the archive. No company, editor or algorithm publishes a recording on its own: every recording waits in Ikpe until the people — especially kin from the same hometown — decide it is ready. It turns the Igbo tradition of consensus-based decisions into the archive's quality and consent check.

Rules to settle Validate: who counts as kin and how that is verified; how the quorum size is set per hometown; what share of Keep votes passes a recording; how long voting stays open; how a "No" is handled; and how to protect small hometowns with few members.

06How each person uses it

A facilitator's session

  1. Gets set up. Signs up through Ọha or a hometown union, completes short training, receives or uses a tablet/phone.
  2. Arranges a visit. Agrees a time with the elder and family; chooses a quiet room.
  3. Opens a session. Enters the elder's name, town, age and dialect; the checklist confirms room and mic level.
  4. Takes consent. Reads the ownership explanation aloud; the elder agrees and chooses who may hear the recording.
  5. Picks a topic. Story, proverb, song, recipe, craft or place-lore.
  6. Records. Lets the elder lead, pauses when needed, adds notes on names and places mentioned.
  7. Reviews and saves. Plays back, checks tags and the provenance statement, saves to Ọbá. The family receives a copy.
  8. Follows up. A reviewer transcribes and checks it; the facilitator is credited when it goes live.

A learner's day

  1. Joins. Downloads the app, picks their hometown and family dialect, or is added to a family plan by a parent.
  2. Opens the app. Greeted with "Nnọọ, [name]." and today's lesson.
  3. Learns. Hears each word in an elder's voice, says it, sees the tone pattern, gets feedback, tries again if needed.
  4. Reads the proverb of the day. Taps through to hear the elder who shared it.
  5. Explores Ọbá. Listens to stories and songs from their own hometown.
  6. Connects in Ọha. Finds people from the same town; shares a phrase with a grandparent.
  7. Gives back. Nominates their own elder to be recorded — which restarts the loop.

A kin member voting in Ikpe

  1. Is notified. A badge on the Ikpe tab shows a new recording from their hometown is waiting.
  2. Listens. Plays the full recording and reads the elder's details and provenance.
  3. Judges. Votes Keep, Wait or No — and, if Wait or No, says why.
  4. Watches the quorum. Sees how many kin have voted and how close the recording is to a decision.
  5. Sees the outcome. Once passed, the recording moves to Ọbá and may become lessons; if held, the facilitator and family are told what needs fixing.

An elder's experience

  1. Is invited through family, a facilitator or a hometown union — never cold-approached.
  2. Hears plainly that the recording belongs to them and their community, and chooses who may hear it.
  3. Speaks at their own pace, on a subject they choose.
  4. Receives a copy for the family and is named whenever the recording is used.
  5. Can change visibility or withdraw at any time through the facilitator or family.

A parent or family organiser

  1. Sets up a family plan and adds children.
  2. Sees each child's progress and the words they have learnt.
  3. Arranges for a grandparent to be recorded so the children learn from their own family's voice.

What is never for sale: elder recordings, personal data, or access to the archive. The archive is not monetised, licensed to third parties, or used to train outside AI models without the explicit consent of elders and their communities.

Where the money goes

  • Field work — facilitator training and stipends, travel, recording kits.
  • Archive care — transcription, review, translation, long-term storage and backup.
  • Product — the apps, pronunciation-feedback model, content design.
  • Community & brand — social, events, partnerships.

10Brand

The brand is the methodology made visible: warm, slow, specific, credited.

  • Voice — a patient relative talking to a younger relative. Reverent and warm, with moments of encouragement. No hype, no emoji in our own copy, Igbo never italicised, diacritics never stripped.
  • Colour — clay (brand), marigold (accent), forest (archive), plum (community), on warm bone and cream neutrals.
  • Type — Outfit for display, DM Sans for body, Fraunces reserved for elders and archive content.
  • Mark — two arcs meeting the dot-below diacritic; abstract and original, deliberately not a traditional motif.

11Operations

Facilitator network

Facilitators are community volunteers — often a grandchild, niece or neighbour — trained to record respectfully. They complete a short training covering consent, recording technique, metadata and cultural protocol, then use Facilitator Mode on a tablet or phone. Quality is checked by a reviewer before anything is published.

Content pipeline

  1. Recording uploaded with consent and metadata.
  2. Transcription and translation by a qualified speaker of that dialect.
  3. Staff check for technical quality and obvious sensitivity issues.
  4. Ikpe: kin from the hometown vote Keep, Wait or No until quorum is reached.
  5. Publication to Ọbá at the elder's chosen visibility — or back to the facilitator with the council's reasons.
  6. Selection of suitable material for lessons and social posts — always attributed.

Team roles (initial)

Founder / director · product & engineering lead · linguist / content lead · community & facilitator lead · social & brand manager · part-time reviewers and transcribers per dialect. Validate against actual team and budget.

12Ethics & governance

For a preservation project, trust is the product. These rules are non-negotiable.

  • Consent & co-ownership. Every elder signs a consent record that names them as co-owner of their recordings and lets them change visibility or withdraw.
  • Attribution. Elders are always named with place and dialect. No anonymous "an elder said".
  • Minors. Learner stories involving under-18s require written guardian permission, named in the caption.
  • Cultural IP. Uli, nsibidi, akwete and other traditional visual forms are not used in the brand. If they are ever used, they are commissioned from Igbo artists with credit and payment.
  • No synthetic likeness. No AI-generated Igbo faces or voices presented as real people.
  • Dialect equity. No dialect is presented as "correct". Gaps in representation are reported openly.
  • Ikpe — community judgement. No recording is published without passing the kin council. The company does not overrule a council "No"; only the elder and family can override, and only to restrict.
  • Elder council. An advisory council of elders from several states reviews voice, naming, Ikpe rules and sensitive content before launch and at least yearly.
  • Data. Recordings stored with redundant backups; personal data minimised; clear privacy policy compliant with Nigeria's data protection law and those of diaspora markets (e.g. GDPR). Validate with counsel.
